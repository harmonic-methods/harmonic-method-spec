The Orchestrator plans who will do the work, what they need, and which dependencies must be resolved first. The Conductor uses that plan to lead day-to-day execution, resolve blockers within agreed authority, and escalate changes to scope or capacity.

The practices below can be supported by a shared task board, documents, or a coordination tool. Use enough detail to prevent duplicate work and lost context.

<span id="forces" aria-hidden="true" />

## People and AI agents [#people-and-agents]

Choose who can perform each planned change based on the skills, context, access, and review it needs. Name a person who remains accountable when an AI agent assists.

**Example:** An agent drafts a change to the estimate status display. A developer reviews the implementation, and the designated approver confirms that it represents their decisions correctly. Give the agent the Beat Version, relevant Notes, allowed actions, and acceptance criteria before it starts.

## Group related work [#sections]

Group planned changes by the responsibility they serve, such as the customer interface, approval rules, or reporting. If a change touches several areas, identify the reviewers and dependencies rather than treating each area as unrelated work.

## Assign an owner [#assignment]

Record who is responsible for seeing a planned change through to acceptance. That responsibility remains clear when someone else contributes or temporarily takes over the work.

**Example:** The developer owns the status-display change even while an agent drafts it. The owner arranges review, follows up on corrections, and confirms the acceptance criteria are met.

## Show who is working now [#claim]

Ownership and current activity answer different questions. Before starting, make it visible that someone is working on the item so another person or agent does not duplicate it.

Use an in-progress status with a named contributor, or a short update in the shared work record. When work stops or changes hands, update that signal so it stays useful. The specific tool is your choice.

## Pass on the context [#handoff]

A handoff should include the planned change, its linked Beat Version if any, the current output, decisions and constraints, checks already run, and unresolved questions. Name the next person or agent and the action expected of them.

**Example:** “The status display is ready for review. Pending, granted, and declined are covered; the no-decision case still needs confirmation. Review the approver's Note before changing that behavior.”

## Sequence dependencies [#sequencing]

Record what must be available before another planned change can proceed. Where possible, agree on an interface or input that lets work continue independently.

**Example:** Confirm the approval states before implementing the display. Once those states are agreed, the interface and reporting work can proceed in parallel. If the states change, the Conductor checks the impact and escalates any change beyond the agreed plan.

## Keep coordination current [#why-explicit]

During execution, the Conductor checks for blocked items, out-of-date activity updates, missing handoff context, and decisions that affect other work. Update the shared record when something changes. Bring capacity or sequencing changes beyond the agreed plan back to the Orchestrator.

## Coordinate across systems [#across-systems]

Use these same practices when work spans systems or teams. Share the relevant Codas, plans, Notes, owners, and dependencies so a decision in one system reaches the others it affects. A handoff should identify who needs the information and what they must do with it.

Conductor coordinates execution within agreed boundaries; changes to the arrangement or allocation beyond those boundaries go to Orchestrator. Conflicts with capability definitions, investment, or strategic direction go to the corresponding accountable role. [See the role boundaries](/docs/method/roles).
