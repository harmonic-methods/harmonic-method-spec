The Producer decides whether work is worth the resources it requires. That includes choosing what to start, reviewing work already underway, and making an explicit decision when its expected value changes.

The question is: **Given what we know now, is this the best use of the next investment?**

## Review the investment case [#question]

Before committing more time or money, ask:

1. **Who benefits, and how?** Name the outcome: fewer approval delays, less manual handling, lower operational risk, or another result the people affected recognize.
2. **What evidence supports it?** Review user feedback, usage, observed outcomes, a demo, or a small trial. Separate what has been demonstrated from what is still assumed.
3. **What will it cost from here?** Include remaining delivery effort, support, maintenance, adoption work, and the work it would displace.
4. **What are the alternatives?** Consider a smaller change, an existing tool, a manual process, deferral, or stopping.
5. **What would change the decision?** Identify the assumption to test, an acceptable investment limit, and the evidence or event that should trigger another review.

<CalloutContainer type="warning" role="note" aria-label="Warning: Past spending does not justify more investment">
  <CalloutTitle>Warning: Past spending does not justify more investment</CalloutTitle>
  <CalloutDescription>Compare the remaining cost with the expected benefit and alternatives. Continuing needs a current reason, even when the team has already invested heavily.</CalloutDescription>
</CalloutContainer>

For required work, such as meeting a contractual or regulatory obligation, compare viable ways to satisfy the constraint. Stopping cannot mean silently abandoning the obligation.

## Choose an action [#decisions]

| Decision | When it fits | What to make explicit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fund** | The expected benefit justifies a bounded first investment, or a small trial can resolve a consequential uncertainty. | What is funded, the limit, the evidence expected, and who owns the next step. |
| **Continue** | Evidence still supports the outcome and the remaining investment is justified against alternatives. | What has been learned, what remains, and when to review again. |
| **Reshape** | The outcome still matters, but the current scope, approach, or cost no longer makes sense. | What changes, what is excluded, and which commitments need to be renegotiated. |
| **Stop** | The benefit no longer justifies further investment, another option is better, or a critical assumption has failed. | What ends, why, and how users, obligations, and ongoing support will be handled. |

## Examples at capability and system scope [#altitudes]

These examples are hypothetical; they illustrate decisions rather than measured results.

### Fund a small approval trial

A team proposes client estimate approval. Interviews suggest that email follow-ups delay decisions, but the benefit of a new workflow is uncertain.

Producer funds a limited trial with a few clients, with a cap on effort. The team will compare approval delays and manual follow-up work with the current process before asking for a larger investment.

### Continue when evidence supports the next step

Suppose the trial is being used and shows fewer follow-ups, while approval history remains incomplete. Producer reviews the evidence and the cost of finishing that capability. If the remaining investment is justified, fund the next bounded step and define the evidence to review afterward.

### Reshape an approach that is becoming too expensive

Suppose every client requests a custom approval path. Delivery and support costs are growing faster than the expected benefit.

Producer asks for options within a smaller investment limit. Composer defines a simpler capability around a shared approval path; Orchestrator revises the delivery arrangement. Exceptional cases may stay manual if that is acceptable. The decision preserves the useful outcome while changing how much is being funded.

### Stop a duplicate reporting system

Suppose a second reporting system has little use and an existing service already meets the need. Producer compares the remaining cost with migration and retirement costs, then decides whether to stop further development.

Stopping includes an agreed transition for users, data, and support. Record the decision so the same proposal is not restarted later without addressing its rationale.

## Compare competing investments [#portfolio]

Across several systems, consider what each option displaces. A useful improvement can still be a lower priority than fixing a costly operational failure elsewhere.

Build a shared view of the capabilities before choosing among investments:

- Map what each system enables and where capabilities overlap or leave gaps.
- Trace the proposed outcomes to the strategic direction. Surface conflicts instead of optimizing each system in isolation.
- Identify cross-system dependencies and what each option would delay or displace.

Ask Orchestrator for realistic capacity and dependency options, Composer for scope alternatives, Conductor for delivery evidence, and Promoter for adoption evidence. Compare the options against the strategic priorities and make the tradeoff visible.

Director owns strategic direction and whether its assumptions still hold. Producer judges investment within that direction. Evidence that challenges the strategy goes to Director; a funding decision should not quietly redefine it.

## Record the decision and revisit it [#standing]

Capture the choice and rationale in a decision Note attached to what it informs. Include the evidence, assumptions, investment limits, accountable person, and review trigger. Confirm that the plan and owners reflect the decision.

Review at the agreed cadence and when material evidence changes—for example, a cost increase, failed trial, changed customer need, or new alternative. Use [Downbeats](/docs/method/cadence#downbeat) as preserved capability evidence alongside cost and adoption information.

Measures such as Value Velocity can support the review where an implementation defines them and has reliable inputs. The method does not prescribe a universal formula or threshold. A quality score describes the statement or plan assessed; investment judgment still needs evidence about expected value and cost.
